We’ve covered your Point and the center it lives in. Both are useful maps of how you tend to create. But there’s a question underneath both of them that’s gotten a lot louder this year: if a machine can now produce a passable poem, a competent business plan, or a decent first draft in seconds, what’s actually left that’s yours?

The gap between output and origin

A generated piece of writing and a written piece of writing can look nearly identical on the page. The difference isn’t in the artifact, it’s in where it came from. A machine’s output is a recombination of what already exists, optimized for plausibility. A human’s creative act starts somewhere else: a specific tension the person is carrying, a question they haven’t resolved, a version of themselves they’re trying to become through the work.

That origin point is exactly what the Enneagram maps. Your Point isn’t a style preference, it’s the particular tension you keep returning to, whether you’re building a product, leading a team, or writing a chapter. A Point 4 creating from longing produces something no averaging of internet text will produce, because the longing is theirs, not a statistical pattern. A Point 5 creating from the need to understand something fully, for the first time, is doing something a system trained to predict the next likely word cannot do: caring whether the understanding is real.

Center tells you how, Point tells you why

If the three centers (Body, Heart, Head) describe your instinctive mode of creating, and your Point describes the specific tension driving it, together they describe something a machine has neither of: a stake in the outcome. Body-center creators aren’t just fast, they’re risking something physical and immediate. Heart-center creators aren’t just emotionally attuned, they’re exposed, because the work is entangled with who they are. Head-center creators aren’t just thorough, they’re trying to resolve real uncertainty about something they don’t yet know.

None of that is available to a system with no body, no attachment, and nothing at stake in whether the work lands. It can imitate the shape of all three. It cannot originate any of them.

Where this leaves your work

This isn’t an argument against using new tools, plenty of creative people already use them well, for research, for a rough first pass, for getting unstuck. It’s an argument for knowing exactly what you’re not outsourcing when you do: the tension, the stake, the reason the work needed to exist in the first place. That part still has to come from you, and understanding your Point and center is one of the clearest ways to find it on purpose instead of by accident.

This is the central question Russ and I kept returning to while writing The Creative Moment: not whether machines can generate convincing creative output (they clearly can), but what remains distinctly, unautomatably human about the act of creating. The Enneagram, it turns out, is one of the oldest maps we have for answering that.
