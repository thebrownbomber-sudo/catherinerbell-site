There's a voice in almost every creator's ear these days, and it speaks the language of efficiency. It knows what performs well. It knows what the algorithm rewards. It knows how to trim the fat, hit the beats, deliver value. And it's not wrong, exactly. These are real considerations if you want your work to reach people.

But there's another part of you, quieter and more stubborn, that refuses to listen. It's the part that wants to linger on a phrase that doesn't serve the argument. The part that keeps circling back to an image that doesn't fit the brief. The part that feels a strange loyalty to something in the work that you can't justify, that you might even be a little embarrassed by.

This is the part I want to talk about. Because I think it's the part that makes the work actually yours.

The logic of optimization

Optimization isn't new, but it's gotten louder. We now have tools that can tell us, with apparent precision, what's working and what isn't. Word counts, engagement rates, attention curves. The feedback loop is tighter than it's ever been, and there's a seductiveness to it. Why wouldn't you want to know what resonates?

The problem isn't the data itself. It's what happens when the data becomes the only criteria. When you start writing (or painting, or composing, or building) with one eye on the dashboard, something subtle shifts. The locus of authority moves outside you. You're no longer asking "What does this piece need?" You're asking "What do they want?" And those are different questions.

Machines are very good at the second question. They can analyze patterns in what has worked before and generate outputs that fit those patterns. This is genuinely useful for certain tasks. But it's also a kind of averaging. It tends toward the center of the distribution, toward what's already been rewarded. The stubborn, unjustifiable part of you, the part that refuses to optimize, is precisely what sits outside that distribution.

Where the refusal lives in the centers

If you've been following these posts, you know the Enneagram maps three centers of intelligence: Body, Heart, and Head. Each has its own way of knowing, its own relationship to creative work. And each has its own version of this refusal.

In the Body center (Points 8, 9, 1), the refusal often shows up as a visceral no. Something in the gut rejects the optimized path before the mind can articulate why. Point 8 might feel it as a kind of contempt for being managed by metrics. Point 1 might experience it as a stubborn insistence that the "right" version of the work isn't the one that tests best. Point 9 might simply go still and quiet when pushed toward efficiency, a passive resistance that's easy to mistake for laziness but is actually a deep unwillingness to betray something essential.

In the Heart center (Points 2, 3, 4), the tension is more complicated, because the Heart is already oriented toward how the work lands with others. Point 3, especially, can feel torn: the pull toward optimization is strong, because it promises recognition and success. But there's often a moment, late at night or in the shower, when Point 3 knows that the optimized version isn't the real one. Point 4 may feel the refusal as a kind of protective pride: "I won't dilute this just because it's too strange." Point 2 might notice it as a reluctance to perform helpfulness when the work wants to do something else entirely.

In the Head center (Points 5, 6, 7), the refusal can get tangled with analysis. Point 5 might intellectualize its resistance, finding reasons why optimization is philosophically bankrupt (while secretly just wanting to protect the weird thing they are making). Point 6 might worry that refusing to optimize is irresponsible, impractical, even dangerous; but underneath that worry is often a loyalty to something the data can't see. Point 7 might resist optimization simply because it's boring, because following the formula means giving up the thrill of the unexpected turn.

The point isn't that any of these responses is better than another. It's that the refusal shows up differently depending on where you live in the system. And recognizing your own version of it can help you stop pathologizing it.

What the refusal is protecting

When you feel that stubborn pull toward something in your work that doesn't make sense, that doesn't fit, that you can't optimize, what exactly is being protected?

I think it's the part that wasn't calculated. The part that emerged from your actual encounter with the material, not from your anticipation of how it would be received. The part that carries your fingerprint, not because you tried to be original, but because you followed something true.

This is different from mere self-indulgence, though it can be hard to tell the difference from the inside. Self-indulgence is attachment to the feeling of being special. The refusal I'm describing is loyalty to something in the work itself, something you didn't put there on purpose, something that showed up because you were paying attention.

Machines can mimic many things, but they can't have this kind of loyalty. They don't encounter material; they process inputs. They don't follow something true; they optimize for objectives. The refusal is a marker of human presence in the work, the trace of a person who was actually there, wrestling with something that didn't come easily.

The risk of listening

None of this is meant to suggest that you should ignore all feedback, never consider your audience, or treat every stubborn impulse as sacred. Sometimes the refusal is just ego. Sometimes the unjustifiable thing really should be cut. Discernment is required, and discernment is hard.

But I notice that the cultural pressure right now runs almost entirely in one direction. The message is: optimize, test, iterate, ship. There's very little encouragement to linger, to protect the strange thing, to trust that what you can't justify might be what matters most. So I'm offering a small counterweight.

The next time you feel that stubborn part of yourself refusing to get with the program, consider the possibility that it's not a flaw to be managed. It might be the part of you that knows something the dashboard doesn't. It might be the part that makes the work worth making at all.
